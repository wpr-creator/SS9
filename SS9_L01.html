Who gets to tell the story of America, and what happens when whole communities are left out?

In 1970, a young woman named Dolores Huerta stood in front of a crowd of farmworkers in California and said something that people were not used to hearing: that their story deserved to be told. Most of these workers had grown up in schools where their families, their languages, and their history were never mentioned. It was as if they did not exist in the story of this country. This course exists because that invisibility has a real cost — and because stories that get left out do not disappear. They wait.

◆

What Is Ethnic Studies?

Ethnic studiesThe study of the histories, cultures, and experiences of communities of color, especially those whose stories have been left out of standard history classes. is not just another history class. It is a course that asks a bigger question: whose experiences get included in the story of a country, and whose get pushed to the side?

In a typical history class, you might spend weeks on presidents, wars, and laws. That information matters. But a lot of people lived through those same events and never got mentioned. Farmworkers, enslaved people, immigrants, and Indigenous communities all shaped this country. Their voices and choices helped build what America is today. Ethnic studies brings those stories back into the room.

This course focuses on four big ideas: identityThe way a person understands who they are, including their family background, culture, language, and community., power, narrativeThe story that gets told about a group of people or a historical event, and who decides what that story says., and resistance. Those four words will come up again and again all semester. Together, they help explain how communities have survived, fought back, and shaped the world around them.

Think about a story you know about your own family or community. Now think: where did you learn that story? Was it in a classroom, or somewhere else? That question is exactly what this course is about.

Who Decides the Story?

Every textbook is a set of choices. Someone decided what to include and what to leave out. Someone chose which events were important enough to get a full chapter and which ones got one sentence or none at all.

For most of American history, those decisions were made by people who did not represent the full range of communities in this country. That meant the stories of Black Americans, Latino communities, Native peoples, and Asian Americans were often shrunk down, simplified, or skipped entirely. This is called representationWhether a group of people is included and accurately shown in media, textbooks, or public life.: who gets to appear in the story, and how they are shown when they do appear.

When students only see certain kinds of people in their textbooks, it sends a message. It says: some lives matter more than others. Some histories are worth studying. Others are not. Those messages affect how students see themselves and how they see each other.

Changing what is taught in schools is not a simple task. In 2021, California passed a law making ethnic studies a required course for all high school students. That law came after years of organizing by students, teachers, and community members who argued that schools had an obligation to tell a fuller, more honest story. You are here, in part, because of that work.

Four Communities, One Course

California's ethnic studies framework centers on four communities. Each one has a long, complex history in this country. Each one has faced serious harm. And each one has organized, created, resisted, and built. This course will study all four.

Native American communities were the first peoples of this land. Their nations had governments, languages, and complex societies long before European settlers arrived. This course will look at what was taken from them and how their communities have fought to survive and reclaim their identity.

African American communities built this country's economy under slavery, then spent generations fighting for the rights and recognition they were always owed. From the Harlem Renaissance to the civil rights movement to Black Lives Matter, Black Americans have never stopped demanding a full place in American life.

Latino and Chicano communities have deep roots in California that go back centuries. Many of today's California cities sit on land that was once part of Mexico. This course will trace the history of labor, immigration, and resistance that has shaped the Latino experience here and across the country.

Asian American and Pacific Islander communities have been part of California's story since the Gold Rush. They built railroads, farmed the land, fought in U.S. wars, and were still told they did not belong. Their story is one of contribution, exclusion, and persistence.

All four of these communities have something in common: their stories were part of America from the beginning. They just were not always included in the books.

Your Story Matters

This course is not just about other people's stories. It is about yours too. Every student in this room has a communityA group of people connected by shared culture, background, neighborhood, or experience.. Every student has a history that stretches back further than they know.

One of the goals of this course is to help you see that your background is not separate from history. It is part of history. The choices your family made, the places they came from, the languages they spoke: all of it connects to larger events and forces that shaped this country and the world.

This course will ask you to think, write, and talk about your own identity. That can feel uncomfortable sometimes. It can also feel powerful. You do not need to share anything you do not want to share. But we will ask you to think carefully about who you are and where your story fits in the bigger picture.

"We didn't cross the border. The border crossed us."

Chicano community saying, widely used during the Chicano Movement, late 1960s–1970s.

This phrase captures the experience of Mexican American families in the Southwest who found themselves on the U.S. side of the border after the Mexican-American War of 1848, without ever leaving their homes.

🏠 Local History Connection: San Diego and the Border

San Diego sits fewer than 20 miles from Tijuana, Mexico. This region was part of Mexico until the Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo in 1848. Many families in this area have roots on both sides of a border that was drawn through their homeland. The Kumeyaay people, whose territory spanned what is now San Diego County and northern Baja California, still live on both sides of that line today.

The Kumeyaay Nation has 12 federally recognized bands in San Diego County alone. Their history is one of the oldest and deepest connections to this land in the region.

How This Still Matters Today

The fight over whose story gets told in schools is happening right now, in classrooms across the country.

Debates about what to teach in history class are not new. But in the last few years, they have become a major national argument. Some states have moved to ban or restrict the teaching of ethnic studies. Others, like California, have made it a graduation requirement. The question of whose story belongs in a classroom is one of the most contested questions in American education right now.

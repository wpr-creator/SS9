Chicano and Latino Identity:
Border, Culture, and Power

Essential Question

How has the history of the U.S.-Mexico border shaped the lives, culture, and political power of Latino communities?

In 1848, a man named Pablo de la Guerra stood up in the California State Senate and said something the new American government did not want to hear. He had been born in Santa Barbara when it was part of Mexico. He had never moved. But now, without crossing any border, he was suddenly a foreigner in his own home. "I am a stranger in my native land," he said. He was not speaking for himself alone. He was speaking for the tens of thousands of Mexican families who woke up one morning in 1848 and found that a war they had not started had redrawn the map around them.

I

The Border Moved Over People, Not the Other Way Around

The Treaty of Guadalupe HidalgoThe 1848 peace agreement that ended the Mexican-American War and transferred California, Texas, New Mexico, Arizona, Nevada, Utah, and parts of Colorado from Mexico to the United States., signed in 1848, ended the Mexican-American War. With it, Mexico gave up — or was forced to give up — over half its national territory. California, Texas, New Mexico, Arizona, Nevada, Utah, and parts of Colorado all became part of the United States in a single document. The price the U.S. paid was 15 million dollars. At the time of the transfer, approximately 100,000 Mexican citizens were living in that territory. They did not immigrate to the United States. The United States immigrated to them.

The treaty promised these residents full citizenship and the protection of their property rights. Both promises were broken almost immediately. Mexican landowners in California lost their land through a combination of legal costs, court manipulation, new property taxes, and outright violence. Within 20 years of the treaty, most of the large rancho land grants that had belonged to Mexican California families had passed into the hands of Anglo American settlers. The legal system designed to protect Mexican property rights was the same system that dismantled them.

This history is the foundation of everything that follows. The idea that Mexican Americans are immigrants in a foreign country erases the fact that their families were often in this territory before the United States was. The popular saying "We didn't cross the border; the border crossed us" is not a slogan. It is a precise description of what the Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo actually did.

California had been part of Spain and then Mexico for over 250 years before 1848. The place names, the architecture, the irrigation systems, the cattle ranching culture, the entire agricultural foundation of the state: all of it was built on Mexican and Indigenous labor and knowledge. That history did not disappear in 1848. It just stopped being acknowledged.

"We are now Americans, but we are not treated as Americans. We are strangers in our own land."

Pablo de la Guerra, California State Senate, speech on the Land Act, 1856.

De la Guerra was a California state senator and former Mexican citizen who watched his community's land and legal rights disappear in the years after the Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo — he spent years arguing in court and in the legislature that promises made in the treaty were being systematically broken.

II

Labor, Exploitation, and Deportation

When the United States entered World War II in 1941, millions of American men went overseas to fight. That left an enormous gap in the agricultural workforce. California's farms — which fed much of the country — suddenly had no one to work them. The U.S. government's solution was the Bracero ProgramA guest worker agreement between the U.S. and Mexico from 1942 to 1964 that brought over 4 million Mexican workers to the U.S. on temporary contracts, mostly for farm labor., a formal agreement with Mexico that brought temporary workers north under contract to harvest crops.

Between 1942 and 1964, over 4 million Mexican workers came to the United States through the Bracero Program. They picked cotton, harvested citrus, cut sugar beets, and planted and pulled vegetables from California to Texas to Michigan. Without them, American grocery shelves during and after the war would have looked very different. These workers were promised fair wages, decent housing, and safe conditions. Most of those promises were not kept. Workers were charged for food and housing that ate up most of their wages. Many were exposed to dangerous pesticides with no protection. Some were promised savings accounts that the companies administering the program never paid out.

Then, in 1954, the U.S. government launched a campaign called Operation Wetback. Despite the violent slur in its name, this was official U.S. government policy. Over 1 million people were deportedForced out of a country by the government; in U.S. history, deportation has repeatedly targeted Mexican and Latino communities, including many U.S. citizens and legal residents. or pressured to leave, often without any legal process. Many of those deported were legal residents. Some were U.S. citizens. Entire families were separated. The same government that had welcomed Mexican labor when it was needed had decided, once again, that Mexican people were not welcome when the work was done.

This pattern: recruit when needed, exploit while here, deport when convenient: has repeated itself across American history. The Bracero Program was not an isolated event. It was part of a longer story about how the U.S. economy has used Mexican and Latino labor while denying the people who provide it the full rights and dignity they deserve.

Between the 1930s and the 1950s, an estimated 1.8 million people of Mexican descent were deported or forced out of the United States during two separate mass removal campaigns: the 1930s repatriation drives and Operation Wetback. Historians estimate that up to 60 percent of those removed during the 1930s campaign were U.S. citizens. Their citizenship did not protect them. The color of their skin and the sound of their last name did.

Today, deportation remains a central feature of the U.S. immigration debate. The communities, families, and legal battles that make that debate so personal trace directly back to these decades. Understanding the Bracero Program and Operation Wetback is not just history. It is context for conversations happening in the news right now.

III

The Chicano Movement: What Was Demanded and What Was Won

In 1965, a soft-spoken organizer named Cesar Chavez called a strike against grape growers in Delano, California. Farmworkers: mostly Filipino and Mexican: walked off the fields and demanded fair wages, safe conditions, and the right to organize a union. What followed was a five-year struggle that included a nationwide grape boycott, hunger strikes, and marches of hundreds of miles. In 1970, the growers finally signed contracts. The United Farm Workers union had won.

That victory was part of a larger explosion of organizing called the Chicano MovementA political and cultural movement of the 1960s and 1970s in which Mexican Americans organized for civil rights, fair labor conditions, educational equality, and political power, and embraced the term "Chicano" as a statement of pride and identity.. In 1968, over 10,000 Chicano students in East Los Angeles walked out of five high schools to protest unequal education, under-resourced schools, and counselors who discouraged students from going to college. These walkouts, called the Blowouts, were among the first large student strikes in U.S. history. They forced the Los Angeles school district to negotiate and helped launch a national movement for bilingual education.

The Chicano Movement also reclaimed identity. Mexican Americans had long been pressured to downplay their heritage: to Anglicize their names, speak only English, and distance themselves from their cultural roots. The movement said the opposite. Activists embraced the word "Chicano," reclaimed Indigenous heritage as part of their identity, created murals that covered entire city walls with Mexican and pre-Columbian imagery, and argued that their culture was a strength, not something to hide.

Here is what the Chicano Movement demanded and what changed:

01
Farm Worker Rights

The United Farm Workers won the first union contracts for agricultural workers in California. The California Agricultural Labor Relations Act of 1975 gave farmworkers the legal right to organize for the first time.

02
Bilingual Education

The Bilingual Education Act of 1968 and subsequent California legislation required schools to provide instruction in students' home languages. It was a direct response to Chicano student organizing.

03
Political Representation

The Chicano Movement sparked a generation of Latino political organizing. MALDEF, the Mexican American Legal Defense Fund, was founded in 1968 and has fought for Latino civil rights in court ever since.

04
Cultural Reclamation

Chicano muralism, literature, and theater created a new public identity. Artists like Luis Valdez and painters of the mural tradition made Chicano history visible on the walls of cities across the Southwest.

"We shall overcome. Yes, we will, but only if we respect that most precious possession of our people: their faith, their hope, and their strength as a movement."

Cesar Chavez, statement ending his 25-day hunger strike, March 1968.

Chavez fasted for 25 days in 1968 to recommit the farmworker movement to nonviolent organizing at a moment when many younger activists were pushing for more confrontational tactics — this statement, delivered to a crowd of thousands that included Robert F. Kennedy, became one of the defining words of the Chicano Movement.

IV

Latino Power in California Today

Today, Latinos are the largest ethnic group in California. Over 15 million Latinos live in the state, making up approximately 40 percent of the total population. They are the fastest-growing group of eligible voters in the country. In California, Latino political representation has grown significantly in recent decades: the state legislature, the courts, and local government all reflect that shift, though representation still does not match population numbers.

The immigration debate in the United States today is inseparable from this history. The DACADeferred Action for Childhood Arrivals — a 2012 executive order that protected people brought to the United States as children from deportation and allowed them to work legally; its legal status has been in court battles ever since. program, created in 2012, has protected approximately 800,000 young people — often called Dreamers — from deportation. Most of them grew up entirely in the United States. They went to American schools, speak English, and have built their entire lives here. Their immigration status, a consequence of decisions made by their parents when they were children, remains unresolved by Congress more than a decade later.

The undocumented immigrant population in the United States is estimated at approximately 11 million people. The majority come from Mexico and Central America. Most have lived in the United States for ten or more years. They pay taxes, start businesses, raise citizen children, and contribute to communities across the country. The political debate about their status rarely reflects the actual human complexity of their lives.

California has responded by extending certain state protections to undocumented residents: state financial aid for college, driver's licenses, and limitations on cooperation between local police and federal immigration enforcement. These are political choices that trace directly back to the Chicano Movement's demand that Latino communities be treated as full members of the society they have always helped build.

🏠 This Is a San Diego Story

San Diego sits at one of the busiest international border crossings in the world. The San Ysidro Port of Entry processes over 70,000 northbound crossings every single day. The communities of Barrio Logan, Logan Heights, and National City have deep roots in Mexican American and Chicano history. Barrio Logan's Chicano Park, created in 1970 when community members literally sat down in front of bulldozers to reclaim land that had been taken for a highway interchange, is now a National Historic Landmark covered in some of the most significant Chicano murals in the world.

On April 22, 1970, community members and students occupied the land beneath the Coronado Bridge and refused to leave until the city agreed to build a park there. Chicano Park opened the same year. It is located at Crosby Street and National Avenue in Barrio Logan, and you can visit it today.

V

How This Still Matters Today

The border is not a line on a map. It is a living political reality that shapes the daily lives of millions of people in this region right now.

Every debate about immigration enforcement, DACA, the border wall, farmworker wages, and bilingual education connects directly to the history in this lesson. The Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo is still cited in court cases. The legal fights that the Chicano Movement started in the 1960s are still being argued. Deportation remains one of the most contested tools of U.S. government policy. And the question of whether Latino communities will be treated as full members of American society — with the rights, representation, and respect that their history in this land deserves — remains unanswered.

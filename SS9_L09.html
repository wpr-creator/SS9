PHASE ONE

HOW THEY GOT HERE

Filipino — South Bay

Filipino Americans are in San Diego because of 1898 — when the United States paid Spain $20 million to take the Philippines and then fought a brutal war to prevent Filipino independence.

In 1898, the United States took the Philippines from Spain in the Treaty of Paris and then fought a brutal three-year war against Filipino independence fighters to keep it. An estimated 200,000 to 600,000 Filipino civilians died. Filipinos became American nationalsPeople subject to a country's laws and eligible for some of its protections — but not full citizens and not entitled to immigration rights. — under American law, able to serve in the American military, but not citizens and not able to immigrate freely. The U.S. Navy recruited Filipino men as stewards and mess attendants beginning in 1901. By the 1920s, thousands of Filipino Navy families had settled in San Diego, National City, and Chula Vista. Those men were restricted to the steward branch — cooking and cleaning for white officers — until after WWII. San Diego now has the second largest Filipino American population in the country, with multigenerational roots in the South Bay that trace directly back to American imperial decisions made in 1898.

Somali — City Heights

Somali Americans are in San Diego because of 1991 and 1993 — when Somalia's government collapsed and the U.S. intervened militarily, then withdrew without resolving the conflict that created millions of refugees.

The Somali Civil War began in 1991 when the government of Siad Barre collapsed, leaving Somalia without a functioning state and triggering violence that displaced millions of people. The United States intervened militarily in 1993 in what became known as the Battle of Mogadishu — made famous by the book and film "Black Hawk Down" — and then withdrew, leaving the conflict unresolved. Somali refugeesPeople who have been forced to flee their home country because of war, persecution, or natural disaster, and who cannot safely return. were resettled in American cities through the 1990s and 2000s, and San Diego received a significant share. City Heights became the center of San Diego's Somali community — a neighborhood that also hosts Ethiopian, Eritrean, and other East African communities, along with Latino, Southeast Asian, and other immigrant families, making it one of the most linguistically diverse ZIP codes in California.

Hmong — Linda Vista

Hmong Americans are in San Diego because of 1961 to 1975 — a secret CIA war in Laos that recruited Hmong farmers to fight as American proxies, then abandoned them when the U.S. withdrew from Southeast Asia.

The Secret War in Laos ran from 1961 to 1975. It was a CIA operation so classified that Congress was never officially told it was happening. The United States recruited Hmong farmers and their sons to fight a proxy warA conflict in which a larger power funds and supports one side of a local war without directly fighting itself. against communist forces in Laos, paying them, training them, and making them entirely dependent on American support. When the United States pulled out of Southeast Asia in 1975, the Hmong were abandoned. The new communist government treated them as traitors. Hundreds of thousands fled across the Mekong River to refugee camps in Thailand, where many lived for years waiting for resettlement. The United States eventually accepted responsibility and resettled Hmong refugees in cities including Minneapolis, Fresno, Sacramento, and San Diego's Linda Vista neighborhood.

Chaldean — El Cajon

Chaldean Americans are in San Diego because of 2003 — when the U.S. invasion of Iraq destroyed central authority and unleashed sectarian violence that made Iraq's ancient Christian communities into targets.

ChaldeansAn ethnic Semitic people indigenous to Mesopotamia — modern Iraq — who have practiced Christianity since the first century AD and speak Neo-Aramaic, a language descended from the language of Jesus. are an ancient Christian people from Iraq who speak Neo-Aramaic — a language descended directly from Aramaic, the language spoken by Jesus. They had lived in Iraq for millennia. Under Saddam Hussein's secular government, Chaldeans had a degree of protection as a religious minority — not because Hussein was humane, but because his government suppressed the sectarian violence that would otherwise have targeted them. When the United States invaded Iraq in 2003 and central authority collapsed, that protection disappeared. Churches were bombed. Community leaders were kidnapped. Families received death threats. Hundreds of thousands of Chaldean Christians fled Iraq between 2003 and 2010. El Cajon received a large share of these refugees, becoming home to the largest Chaldean community in the United States outside of Detroit — earning the local nickname "Little Baghdad."

DID YOU KNOW

The Hmong had no written language before the 1950s. When Hmong women arrived in refugee camps in Thailand after fleeing Laos in 1975, they began creating what are called paj ntaubPronounced "pa ndau" — Hmong story cloths that use needlework to document history, culture, and personal experience. — story cloths, stitched entirely from memory, documenting the Secret War, the flight through the jungle, the river crossing, and life in the camps. A people with no written language created one of the most detailed historical records of the 20th century using needle and thread. Those cloths are now held in major museum collections around the world.

PHASE TWO

WHAT THEY BUILT

Every community profiled in this lesson built institutions in San Diego under difficult conditions. Filipino Americans built churches, civic organizations, and deep political networks in the South Bay — communities grounded in four generations of Navy families, farmworker activists like Philip Vera Cruz, and the balikbayanFilipino for "returnee" — the large cardboard boxes Filipino Americans ship home to the Philippines filled with goods for family, representing the maintenance of connection across the Pacific. box tradition of maintaining connections across the Pacific. The Somali community in City Heights built mosques, halal markets, tea houses, and East African businesses along University Avenue — creating a neighborhood economy that serves the community and is largely invisible to San Diegans who don't live there. The Hmong community built cultural organizations and language preservation programs in Linda Vista, and Hmong women continued the paj ntaub tradition — creating textiles that documented what no official record acknowledged: the full story of the Secret War and what it cost.

The Chaldean community in El Cajon built Chaldean Catholic churches, community centers, restaurants, and small businesses within years of arriving. Building those institutions was not just practical — it was an act of cultural survival. Chaldeans brought with them liturgical traditions, a liturgical language, and a communal identity that predated the Roman Catholic Church. Preserving those traditions in El Cajon meant preserving something that no longer had a safe home in Iraq. The communities profiled in this lesson did not arrive with nothing. They arrived with everything they could carry — language, faith, skill, memory, and the knowledge of what they had left behind.

PHASE THREE

WHO THEY ARE NOW

The children of these communities are growing up as San Diegans in the fullest sense. Second- and third-generation Filipino Americans in Chula Vista attend local schools, work in local hospitals and military bases, and hold elected office. Young Somali Americans who were born in City Heights and have never been to Somalia are still asked "where are you really from?" — a question that reveals how American society continues to treat some people as permanent foreigners regardless of how long their families have been here. Young Hmong Americans in Linda Vista navigate between the Hmong cultural world of their grandparents and the American world of their schools and workplaces. Young Chaldean Americans in El Cajon hold a grief their parents carried across the ocean: the loss of an ancient homeland that an American military decision made uninhabitable.

ART SPOTLIGHT

Because the Hmong had no written language before the 1950s, there was no established tradition of written history. The paj ntaub story cloths became the primary historical record of the Secret War from the Hmong perspective. When American academics and historians began studying the Secret War in the 1980s and 1990s, the story cloths created by women in refugee camps were among the most detailed and reliable accounts they found — more complete in many ways than declassified government documents. A textile tradition created by women with needles and thread outlasted the secrecy of a superpower.

PRIMARY SOURCE MOMENT

"I've always considered myself first and foremost a worker. I came from the Philippines as a worker. I worked in the fields and in the restaurants and in the salmon canneries. The Filipino people came here to work, and we worked, and then we were told we had no rights. We were told to go back where we came from. But where I came from, America already was. America had been there since 1898. We were already Americans before we got here — or America was already Filipino before we left."

— Philip Vera Cruz, interview, circa 1970s, from "Philip Vera Cruz: A Personal History of Filipino Immigrants and the Farmworker Movement"

WHY THIS MATTERS: Philip Vera Cruz co-founded the United Farm Workers with César Chávez and Dolores Huerta. He is describing something specific to the Filipino American experience: that Filipinos arrived in America already shaped by American colonization. "America was already Filipino before we left" — he is saying that the relationship did not begin with immigration. It began in 1898 when the United States took his country. As you read this, think about how this applies to the other communities in this lesson. Which of them could make a similar argument?

THEN

In 1975, Hmong families crossed the Mekong River into Thailand with what they could carry — often their children and almost nothing else. They had fought for the United States in a war the United States denied was happening. They spent years in refugee camps waiting to find out whether the country that had recruited them would accept responsibility for what followed. Many waited years. Some waited more than a decade.

NOW

Today, Hmong Americans in Linda Vista have built a community that includes cultural organizations, language programs, and a generation of young people who are simultaneously American and deeply connected to a culture that was nearly destroyed by an American military decision. The paj ntaub tradition continues. The story cloths are still being made. The history that a superpower kept secret for years is preserved in needlework, passed from grandmother to grandchild.

CONNECTION

The same story — American decision, displacement, resettlement, rebuilding — runs through every community in this lesson. Filipino Americans in the South Bay trace their roots to 1898. Somali Americans in City Heights trace theirs to 1991 and 1993. Hmong Americans in Linda Vista trace theirs to 1961. Chaldean Americans in El Cajon trace theirs to 2003. San Diego is a record of what America has done in the world. Learning to read that record is what it means to understand the city you live in.

PHASE ONE

EARLY ARRIVALS

The first large wave of Arab immigrationMoving from one country to another to live there permanently. began in the 1880s. Many families came from Greater Syria, a region that included modern Lebanon, Syria, Palestine, and Jordan. Most early arrivals were Christian, though Arab Americans have always included many religions. Some left because of poverty, political pressure, or military service under the Ottoman Empire. Many planned to work for a few years and return, but many stayed and built American neighborhoods.

The first large wave of Arab immigration began in the 1880s. Many families came from Greater Syria. That region included modern Lebanon, Syria, Palestine, and Jordan. Most early arrivals were Christian, though Arab Americans have always included many religions. Some left because of poverty, political pressure, or military service. Many stayed and built new neighborhoods.

Walk through Little Syria around 1905: Arabic newspapers in windows, coffee roasting in stores, families above shops, church bells nearby, and children translating for parents as customers step inside.

Many early Arab immigrants worked as peddlers, carrying fabric, lace, clothing, and household goods from town to town. The work was exhausting, but it helped people earn money, learn English, and build customer networks. Over time, many opened grocery stores, dry goods shops, restaurants, and import businesses. They printed Arabic-language newspapers and formed mutual aid groups that helped new arrivals find jobs and housing. These neighborhoods were not just places to survive. They were places to belong.

Culture traveled with people. Families brought recipes, music, poetry, faith traditions, humor, and strong ideas about hospitality. Food mattered, but so did language, newspapers, business signs, church gatherings, coffeehouses, and storytelling. These were tools for keeping identity alive while adapting to a new country. Arab American history includes discrimination, but it also includes jokes, meals, songs, weddings, arguments, and everyday joy.

A second major wave arrived after 1965, when U.S. immigration law allowed more people from outside Europe to enter. This wave included more Muslims, Palestinians, Yemenis, Egyptians, Iraqis, and families fleeing war or political violence. Arab America became even more diverse in religion, national background, language, and class. No single image can represent all Arab Americans. That is why stereotypes are so harmful: they flatten millions of different lives into one false story.

PHASE TWO

ORGANIZING AND FIGHTING BACK

Arab American organizing did not begin after September 11. In 1980, James Abourezk, a Lebanese American former U.S. senator from South Dakota, founded the Arab American Anti-Discrimination CommitteeA civil rights group founded in 1980 to defend Arab Americans from discrimination.. Abourezk had grown up as the son of Lebanese immigrants in a state with few Arab American political leaders. His organization challenged discrimination in schools, workplaces, media, and government. It gave Arab Americans a national voice when many people only knew them through stereotypes.

Arab American organizing did not begin after September 11. In 1980, James Abourezk founded the Arab American Anti-Discrimination Committee. He was a Lebanese American former U.S. senator from South Dakota. His group fought discrimination in schools, jobs, media, and government. It gave Arab Americans a national voice.

STUDENT CONNECTION: Organizing can look like a protest, but it can also look like students asking a teacher to change an unfair lesson, a family reporting a hate incident, or a community showing up at a school board meeting.

After September 11, the pressure became intense. The government launched Special Registration, a program that required men from many Arab and Muslim-majority countries to check in with immigration officials. Thousands were questioned, detained, or deported. Hate crimes rose, and many families worried about speaking Arabic in public or being stopped at airports. Arab American groups responded with lawsuits, protests, voter registration drives, public education, and coalitionDifferent groups working together toward a shared goal. building with other targeted communities.

After September 11, pressure on Arab Americans became intense. The government required many men from Arab and Muslim-majority countries to register with immigration officials. Thousands were questioned, detained, or deported. Hate crimes rose. Many families worried about speaking Arabic in public or being stopped at airports. Arab American groups responded with lawsuits, protests, voter drives, and public education.

Resistance also happened through culture. Jack Shaheen challenged Hollywood stereotypes. Writers and comedians used stories and jokes to describe family life from the inside. Young people used campus clubs, zines, social media, and spoken word to explain who they were before someone else explained them wrong. That is Ethnic Studies in action: a community studies itself, names the systems around it, and creates its own voice.

This organizing changed public life. More Arab Americans ran for office, registered to vote, spoke at school boards, and built relationships with local governments. In Dearborn, Arab Americans have served as mayor and in Congress. Across the country, Arab American communities have used organizing to answer exclusion with presence. Their message was not only "stop targeting us." It was also "we are already part of this country."

PHASE THREE

COMMUNITY TODAY

Arab American life today is full of culture students may already recognize. It is fresh pita at a bakery, dabke at a wedding, Arabic music in a car, and hummus in grocery stores. It is also poetry, comedy, film, journalism, public service, small businesses, and family networks. Arab American culture is not hidden outside American life. It is part of American life.

ART SPOTLIGHT

Adnan grew up in Beirut writing in French because Arabic had been pushed out of her schooling by colonial rule. Later, she made art in the United States, France, and Lebanon. In some works, she moved away from words and let color carry memory instead. Her art shows identity as layered: Arab, American, Lebanese, French-speaking, English-writing, and still unfinished.

PRIMARY SOURCE MOMENT

"We inherited the Arab image primarily from Europeans in the early days... the travel writers, the artists who fabricated these images... And these images were transmitted and inherited by us. We took them, we embellished them, and as a result we now have a kind of reservoir of images that are false."

Jack G. Shaheen, Reel Bad Arabs: How Hollywood Vilifies a People, documentary transcript, 2006

WHY THIS MATTERS: Shaheen argues that stereotypes do not appear from nowhere. They are repeated, inherited, and made to feel normal. His source helps students see media as evidence of power, not just entertainment.

THEN

Early Arab immigrants built neighborhoods around stores, churches, newspapers, coffeehouses, and family networks. Little Syria shows that Arab American life was already urban, creative, and organized more than a century ago. These communities were not temporary guests. They were building American neighborhoods.

NOW

Today Arab Americans build community through festivals, restaurants, mosques, churches, businesses, films, books, music, comedy, and political organizing. In places like Dearborn, Arab American voters and leaders shape local and national conversations. The community is visible not only in moments of crisis, but in ordinary public life.

CONNECTION

The connection between then and now is community building. Earlier generations created newspapers, stores, and churches so people could survive and belong. Today, Arab Americans continue that work through culture, art, voting, organizing, and family life. Their history shows that American identity is built by communities that keep adding new sounds, foods, languages, and stories to the country.
